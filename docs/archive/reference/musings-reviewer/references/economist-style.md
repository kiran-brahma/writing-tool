# Word and Prose Reference

Condensed from KB's Economist Style Guide notes. Applies to both ARGUMENT- and
OBSERVATION-type musings. Sections that don't apply to a given piece (e.g. Business, if
the musing mentions no money or company figures) are skipped silently — don't force them.

## Word choice

Prefer the short, Anglo-Saxon word to the Latinate one: buy over purchase, let over permit,
help over aid, get over obtain, show over demonstrate, give up over relinquish. Test: is it
short, would you say it to a friend, does nearly everyone know it.

Prefer the concrete, plain phrase to the euphemism or abstraction: workers over workforce,
refuses to over demonstrates an unwillingness to, dead civilians over civilian casualties,
lay-offs over redundancies, rape over sexual assault, torture/murder over human-rights
abuses.

Avoid pomposity (summit for meeting, ideation for brainstorming, learnings for lessons),
needless verbing (to action, to gift, to interface, to target, to showcase), and jargon
that obscures more than it reveals (synergy for "fewer workers").

Avoid the banned-word list when a plainer word exists: aspirational, facilitate,
high-profile, iconic, individual (for person), key (as adjective), major, narrative,
paradigm, passionate, proactive, prestigious, stakeholders, surreal, trajectory,
transformative, trigger (verb), vision, and hedge-words used to avoid commitment (rather,
somewhat, possibly, mostly, actually, really).

Avoid dead metaphors and cliches: blue-sky thinking, low-hanging fruit, elephant in the
room, going forward, circle back, game-changer, perfect storm, tipping point, paradigm
shift, wake-up call.

Use adjectives that modify a group rather than a noun that defines a person solely by a
trait: "disabled veterans" over "the disabled", "those with schizophrenia" over
"schizophrenics".

## Concrete vs abstract

Prefer concrete nouns and vivid verbs the reader can picture. Watch for nominalisations
(boring nouns built from verbs — "observation" for "observe") crowding out an actual
subject doing an actual thing. "Grocery bills are growing faster than pay packets" beats
"the rise in salaries is not commensurate with consumer-price inflation."

Don't dilute real crises, scandals, or historic events by calling ordinary things by those
names — the words lose force through over-use.

## Grammar

Prefer the active voice. The passive is acceptable only to: preserve focus on the subject
already established, preserve old-to-new information flow, or when the agent is genuinely
unknown or unimportant. Otherwise, ask who did what to whom.

Short sentences over long ones — each full stop lets the reader's working memory reset.

Who/whom: rephrase as a declaration and see if "he/him" fits; "him" means "whom".
Between you and me, not "between you and I" — prepositions take object pronouns.
Singular subjects take a singular verb.
Items in a list should share the same grammatical form (parallelism).
Prefer the possessive before a gerund where it reads naturally ("his snoring", not "him
snoring").
Avoid dangling modifiers — readers attach a modifier to the nearest noun.
Use singular they for an unspecified antecedent (someone, anyone) rather than he/she.
Non-restrictive clauses should not begin with "that"; restrictive clauses may use "which".
"May" implies real uncertainty; use "might" in past tense for hypotheticals ("he said they
might win", not "may win").

## Punctuation

Full stop is the default. Semicolon connects two independent clauses without and/but.
Colon promises something and then delivers it. Comma is a breathing mark — don't use it to
join two independent sentences. Dash is dramatic — at most one per paragraph, and prefer
avoiding it. Hyphen groups words that act as one unit.

## Numbers

Never open a sentence with a numeral — spell it out or restructure.
Use % not "per cent"; figures for 11 and up.
Month, day, year for dates. Avoid "last week/this year" — give the actual date.
Prefer round fractions to precise percentages where legible ("a third", not "36%").
Give scale for big numbers — per person, per GDP, per some comparable unit — rather than
"eye-watering" or "whopping".
Correlation is not causation — name it explicitly if the piece implies one without ruling
out reverse causation, a third factor, or coincidence.
Statistical significance ≠ effect size ≠ causation — don't conflate them.
Specify mean, median, or mode when using "average"; median is better with outliers.
Percentage change ≠ percentage-point change (going from 7% to 5% is a fall of two
percentage points, not 2%).
Keep significant figures consistent, and don't drop a meaningful trailing zero (8% ≠
8.0%).
Use ratios correctly: "half as tall", not "twice as short" — shortness isn't a measurable
quantity the way height is.

## Business and finance (only when the musing touches money, a company, or the economy)

Gross profit = revenue minus cost of goods sold. Operating profit accounts for wider
running costs. Net income is the full-year bottom line but includes one-off items.
Cashflow ≠ sales. A balance sheet is a snapshot, not a flow.
Always specify which kind of debt (household, corporate, public, total) and whether the
level is worrying given the country's income level — 80% debt-to-GDP means different
things for a rich and a poor country.
Real GDP (inflation-adjusted) for growth claims, not nominal, unless stated.
Spell out financial jargon in plain terms a layperson would understand rather than
assuming the reader already knows it.

## Tightening

Cut "there is/there are" constructions ("sales increased by 23%", not "there was a 23%
increase in sales"). Prefer positive phrasing to double negatives ("it's hard to tell", not
"it is not easy to tell"). Cut adjectives and adverbs that add nothing. Cut common
redundant phrases where a shorter word does the same job (end result → result, absolute
certainty → certainty, the fact that → that).

## Standard of confidence

If a claim is established and sourced, state it plainly — cut "arguably", "some say", "it
might be the case that". If it isn't established, an honest hedge belongs, but don't let
the hedge substitute for actually checking the claim.
